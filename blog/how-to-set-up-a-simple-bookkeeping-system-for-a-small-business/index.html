Simple Bookkeeping for a Small Business: A 20-Minute Weekly System

Most small business owners do not fail at bookkeeping because it is hard. They fail because they let receipts and payments pile up for months, then face a shoebox of paper at tax time. A short, boring weekly habit fixes that. This guide shows a system you can start today with a notebook, a spreadsheet or a printable, and no accounting software.

What you actually need to record

Bookkeeping is only four lists:

  • Money in: date, customer, what it was for, amount, how you were paid.
  • Money out: date, supplier, category, amount, and where the receipt is.
  • Mileage: date, from, to, purpose, distance (if you drive for the business).
  • Invoices: who owes you, how much, due date, and the date it was paid.

If you keep those four lists up to date, you can answer almost any question about your business.

Step 1: Keep business money separate

Use a separate account or at least a separate card for business spending. Mixed money is the single biggest cause of messy books. If you cannot open a new account yet, mark each personal-account transaction that belongs to the business and record only those.

Step 2: Pick 8 to 10 expense categories

Too many categories slow you down. Start with something like supplies, equipment, software, advertising, transport and mileage, phone and internet, fees, training, and other. You can rename them later, but use the same names every month so totals compare.

Step 3: The 20-minute weekly session

Choose one fixed time, for example Friday afternoon, and do this:

  1. Write down every payment received this week (5 minutes).
  2. Write down every business expense and attach or photograph the receipt (8 minutes).
  3. Log any mileage (2 minutes).
  4. Check which invoices are unpaid and send a friendly reminder for any past due (5 minutes).

The rule is that nothing leaves the week unrecorded. Twenty minutes weekly beats five hours at year end.

Step 4: The monthly review

Once a month, add up income and expenses and work out the difference. Then ask three questions: Which customers or products brought in the most? Which cost grew faster than income? Did I set aside money for tax? Put a fixed percentage of every payment aside as you go, using the rate that applies where you live, so the bill never surprises you.

Step 5: A year-end checklist

  • Totals for income and each expense category
  • Total business mileage
  • All receipts filed by month
  • Unpaid invoices listed
  • Start-up costs and large purchases noted
  • Your records handed to an accountant or tax adviser, if you use one

Common mistakes

  • Waiting until the month ends to find receipts.
  • Mixing personal and business spending.
  • Recording income only when the bank shows it, and forgetting cash payments.
  • Not keeping the record of why a mileage trip happened.

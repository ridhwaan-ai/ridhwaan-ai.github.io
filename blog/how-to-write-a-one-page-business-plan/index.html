How to Write a One-Page Business Plan in a Weekend

You do not need a 40-page document to start a small business. You need clear answers to a handful of questions, written down, with real numbers. Here is a weekend method that gets you from a loose idea to a one-page plan you can act on on Monday.

Saturday morning: the idea and the customer

Write one sentence: "I help [who] to [get what result] by [doing what]." If you cannot fill in all three blanks, the idea is still fuzzy. Next, describe your customer as a real person: age range, what they struggle with, where they spend time, what they already pay for. Then talk to three to five people who fit the description. Ask what they do today, what annoys them and what they have paid to fix it. Do not pitch. Listen and write down their exact words.

Saturday afternoon: offer, competitors and price

List what you will sell in plain terms: what the customer gets, how long it takes, and how it is delivered. Then find three competitors and write down what they charge and what they do well. Your job is not to be cheaper; it is to be clearer or more useful for one specific type of customer.

For pricing, work backwards. Add your costs per job or per unit, decide the hourly pay you want, and check the price against what competitors charge. If the numbers do not meet, adjust the offer, not just the price.

Saturday evening: break-even

Break-even tells you how many sales you need before you earn anything. Divide your fixed monthly costs by the profit on each sale. If rent for a stall, software and insurance come to $300 a month and each sale leaves $25 after costs, you need 12 sales a month to break even. Anything above that is profit. Knowing this number removes a lot of guessing.

Sunday morning: money and marketing

List your start-up costs and where the money will come from. Prefer your own savings, pre-orders, a partner or a grant over borrowing; paying interest on a business that has not earned yet adds risk. Then pick two marketing channels, not ten. Choose places where your customer already is, and write the exact message you will use in one sentence.

Finally, plan your first ten customers by name or type. Who will you contact, what will you say and by what date? Ten real conversations beat any amount of branding.

Sunday afternoon: operations, rules and the one-page plan

Write down how a typical day or job will run, which records you need to keep, and what local rules, licences or insurance apply. Rules differ by country and trade, so check with a qualified person. Then copy your answers onto one page: customer, offer, price, break-even, marketing, first ten customers and the three numbers you will track weekly. Add a 30-day action list.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the customer conversations. Guessing is the most expensive step.
  • Pricing from fear. Underpricing hides a business that does not work.
  • Too many channels. Two done well beat six done badly.
  • No review date. Put a 30-day check on the calendar and compare the plan to what happened.

A plan is not a promise; it is a first draft you correct as you learn. Write it this weekend, test it next month and improve it.

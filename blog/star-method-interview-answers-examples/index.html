STAR Method Interview Answers: How to Build Them (With Examples)

"Tell me about a time you handled a difficult colleague." Questions that start like this are called behavioural questions, and they are in almost every interview. The STAR method is the simplest way to answer them clearly. This guide shows how it works, gives a full example, and explains how to prepare a handful of stories that cover most questions.

What does STAR stand for?

  • Situation: the short background. Where were you, and what was going on?
  • Task: what you were responsible for or trying to achieve.
  • Action: what you did, step by step.
  • Result: what happened, ideally with a number, and what you learned.

Interviewers ask behavioural questions because past behaviour is a good guide to future behaviour. STAR keeps you from rambling and makes sure you reach the part they care about: your action and its result.

A worked example

Question: Tell me about a time you had to meet a tight deadline.

Situation: "In my last role in a small retail team, our supplier cancelled a delivery two days before a big sale."

Task: "I was responsible for stock levels on the sale items, so I had to find replacement stock before the weekend."

Action: "I listed the 12 most important products, phoned three other suppliers that afternoon, and agreed a rush order on the eight I could source. I told my manager which four items we could not cover so we could change the display and the signs in time."

Result: "The stock arrived the day before the sale. We sold out of seven of the eight lines, and my manager asked me to write up the process so the team could reuse it."

That answer takes about 60 to 90 seconds. Notice what it does: it names one clear problem, uses "I" for the actions, and ends with a result and a lasting benefit.

Common STAR mistakes

  1. Too much situation. Spend about 20% on Situation and Task together. The Action is the star.
  2. Saying "we" all the time. The interviewer is hiring you. Explain your part honestly while crediting the team.
  3. No result. "And then it was fine" is weak. Give a number, a reaction, or a change that lasted.
  4. Making things up. Only tell true stories. Interviewers often ask follow-up questions, and invented details fall apart quickly.
  5. Only success stories. A mistake you fixed, with what you changed afterwards, is often more convincing than a perfect story.

Prepare six stories, not sixty answers

You do not need a different answer for every question. Prepare six real stories from work, study or volunteering, and you can adapt them to most questions:

  1. A time you solved a problem under pressure
  2. A time you worked with a difficult person or disagreed with someone
  3. A time you made a mistake and fixed it
  4. A time you led, or took the initiative without being asked
  5. A time you learned something quickly
  6. A time you helped a customer, client or colleague in a way that mattered

For each story, write the four STAR lines in one or two sentences, then add one number if you honestly can (hours saved, people helped, money, time, percentage). Practise saying them aloud twice. Reading is not the same as speaking, and the first time you say a story aloud is the time it comes out messy.

Fitting one story to different questions

The "missed delivery" story above could answer "tell me about a deadline", "tell me about a time you solved a problem", or "tell me about a time you communicated bad news". The facts stay the same. You change what you emphasise: speed, problem-solving, or the honest conversation with your manager.

Practise with a tool, then check the facts

Many people now use an AI chat assistant as a practice partner: paste your story, ask it to play the interviewer, and ask for follow-up questions. That can be useful, but be careful. Tell it not to invent details about you, and never repeat an answer you cannot back up in the room. You are the one answering the questions.

Quick checklist before the interview

  • Six STAR stories written down and practised aloud
  • One number in at least four of them
  • Two questions ready to ask the interviewer
  • The job description re-read, with your stories matched to its key skills
  • Your salary range researched, so you are not guessing under pressure

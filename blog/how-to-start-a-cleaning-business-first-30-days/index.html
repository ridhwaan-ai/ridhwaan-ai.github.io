How to Start a Cleaning Business: A Practical First-30-Days Plan

A cleaning business is one of the cheapest to start. You need basic supplies, a way to get to jobs and a reliable routine. Most of the work is in the details: pricing a job so it actually pays you, setting clear expectations with clients and keeping simple records from day one.

This guide covers the first 30 days. It is a planning guide, not legal or tax advice, so check local licensing, insurance and tax rules before you take paid work.

Step 1: Choose what you will clean

Pick one or two services at first so your pricing and checklists stay simple. Common choices are regular home cleaning, deep cleans and move-out cleans, small offices, and vacation rental turnovers between guests. Rental turnovers pay well for the time but demand speed and consistency, so they suit people who like a checklist.

Step 2: Work out your price per hour

Do not copy a competitor's flat price. Decide what you need to earn per hour after costs, then quote from that. A simple method:

  1. Estimate the hours a typical job takes, including travel and set-up.
  2. Add the cost of supplies and fuel for that job.
  3. Divide your total fee by the hours worked to check your real hourly pay.
  4. Raise the price, or narrow the service, if the result is lower than you would accept from an employer.

Time your first few jobs honestly. A flat price that looked fine for a 2-hour job can fall apart when it takes 4.

Step 3: Build room-by-room checklists

Checklists are what make your work consistent and what let you hire help later. Write one for each room type (kitchen, bathroom, living areas, bedrooms) and separate ones for deep cleans, move-outs and rental turnovers. Take a photo of each finished room until you trust your routine, and keep a list of supplies with the cost per job.

Step 4: Find your first clients

Over 30 days, try a small steady routine rather than one big push:

  • Tell neighbours, friends of friends and local community groups what you offer, honestly and without pressure.
  • Contact owners of short-term rentals and small offices with a short, polite message that mentions one thing you can do for them.
  • Offer a fair first-visit price and ask for a review or an introduction if the client is happy.
  • Never send the same message to hundreds of strangers. That is spam and it does not work.

Step 5: Prepare a quote and a first-visit routine

Before each job, ask about the size of the home, the number of bathrooms, pets, any areas to avoid and what a great result looks like. Put the quote in writing, with what is included and what is not. At the first visit, walk through the home with the client and note anything fragile or already damaged.

Step 6: Keep simple records

Record every job, what you were paid, and every cost: supplies, fuel, mileage, equipment. A clear monthly summary lets you see which clients are worth keeping and gives you what you need at tax time. Keep a log of complaints and how you fixed them, because that is how you improve.

Step 7: Grow slowly

When your calendar is steady for a month, raise your prices a little, drop the worst-paying client and think about hiring help. Set a 90-day plan with a client target, an income target and one quality goal.

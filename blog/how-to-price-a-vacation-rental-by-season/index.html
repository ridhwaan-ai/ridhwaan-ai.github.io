How to Price a Vacation Rental: Seasons, Weekends, Gap Nights and Minimum Stays

Price too high and the calendar stays empty. Price too low and you work hard for little profit. The good news is that you do not need a complicated tool to price well. You need a few honest comparisons, a handful of rules and the habit of checking your numbers every month.

This is general information from the seller's own experience of building host tools, not financial advice. Markets differ, so test and adjust for your own area.

Step 1: Find five real comparables

Search your area for places that match yours on sleeping capacity, bedrooms, quality and location. Open the calendar of each one for the next three months and note three things: the nightly rate, the cleaning fee and which dates are already booked. Booked dates tell you what guests actually accept; an empty calendar at a high price tells you what they do not.

Write the five down side by side. Your base rate should land inside the range of the comparables that are booking, not the ones that are sitting empty.

Step 2: Build a base rate and season multipliers

Choose a base nightly rate for an ordinary weeknight in your normal season. Then decide how much more or less to charge in other periods. A simple starting point many hosts use:

  • Low season: a little below base to keep the calendar moving.
  • Shoulder season: base rate.
  • High season and school holidays: a clear step above base.
  • Local events: festivals, graduations, sports weekends and conferences deserve their own higher rate and often a longer minimum stay.

Keep a list of local events for the whole year. Most hosts lose money simply because they forgot a big weekend and sold it at a normal price.

Step 3: Treat weekends and weekdays differently

In many markets Friday and Saturday nights fill first. A modest weekend uplift often pays better than a flat rate. Check your own booking history: if weekends always sell out weeks ahead while Tuesdays stay empty, your weekend price is probably too low or your midweek price too high.

Step 4: Use minimum stays and gap-night rules

A single empty night between two bookings is hard to sell. Two simple rules help:

  • Keep a longer minimum stay on busy weekends and shorter ones for midweek.
  • Offer a small discount on orphan nights, the one or two nights left between bookings, rather than letting them stay empty.

Also remember the cost of every turnover: cleaning, laundry and supplies. A very short stay can look good on the calendar and still earn almost nothing once you subtract those costs.

Step 5: Raise or lower prices based on lead time

If a date is one month away and nobody has looked at it, lower the price a little. If a date is six months away and already booked, you probably started too low next year. Write down what you charged and how far ahead each booking arrived, then use that to set next year's rates.

Step 6: Review monthly and run small experiments

Once a month, check three numbers: how many nights you sold, your average nightly rate and your total income after fees. Change one thing at a time, such as a weekend uplift or a minimum stay, and give it a few weeks before judging it. If you change five things at once you will never know what worked.

Do not forget your costs

A price is only good if it leaves profit. Add up cleaning, supplies, utilities, platform fees and repairs, then compare them with income.

How to Start a Vacation Rental Co-Hosting Business: A Practical First-30-Days Plan

Plenty of people who own a vacation rental do not want to answer guest messages at midnight or schedule cleaners between check-outs. A co-host does that work for a fee. If you are organised, reply quickly and like looking after details, it is one of the lower-cost businesses you can start: you need no property of your own, just reliable systems and a few trusting owners.

This guide walks through the first 30 days. It is a planning guide, not legal advice, so check your local rules before you take on a client.

Step 1: Decide exactly what you will do

"I'll manage everything" sounds attractive but is a trap. Write a short service menu with clear edges. Common building blocks are:

  • Guest messaging and booking questions
  • Calendar and pricing updates
  • Check-in and check-out coordination
  • Cleaning and restocking schedules
  • Small repairs and vendor calls
  • Reviews and monthly owner reports

Start with two or three packages, for example "messaging only", "messaging plus turnovers" and "full service". Note what is not included, such as major repairs, legal disputes or buying supplies out of your own pocket.

Step 2: Price it so it pays you properly

Co-hosts usually charge a percentage of booking revenue, a flat monthly fee, a per-booking fee, or a mix. A percentage feels fair to owners, but check what it works out to per hour. A quiet month on a cheap property can pay you very little for the same messages and turnovers.

Track your real hours for the first few clients, then divide your fee by the hours worked. If the result is below what you would accept from an employer, raise your price or narrow the service. Do the sum before you sign, not after the third month.

Step 3: Choose the owners you want

Not every owner is a good first client. A good one has a property with steady demand, realistic expectations, reasonable house rules and a willingness to answer you within a day. Warning signs include owners who want you to carry costs, change the rules every week or refuse to put anything in writing.

Step 4: Find your first owners

Do not wait for owners to find you. Over 30 days, try a small, steady routine:

  1. Tell people in your area you offer this service, honestly and without pressure.
  2. Look for owners whose listings have slow replies, thin descriptions or few recent reviews, and send a polite, specific note about one thing you could help with.
  3. Join local landlord and rental owner groups, read their rules first and be useful before you mention your services.
  4. Ask every happy owner for an introduction once you have results.

When you reach out, keep it short: who you are, one concrete observation about their listing, and an offer of a no-pressure call. Never send the same message to hundreds of strangers; that is spam and it will not work.

Step 5: Run the first call and put it in writing

On the first call, ask about the property, current bookings, pain points, the house rules, who handles emergencies, and what a great month looks like. Then send a one-page proposal: services, fee, what is excluded, how reports work and how either side can end the arrangement. Have a qualified person review your agreement, and check licensing, insurance and tax rules for your area.

Step 6: Onboard every property the same way

A repeatable onboarding saves you from forgetting things. Cover the property profile, access and lock details, a walk-through with photos, an inventory of supplies, vendor contacts, an emergency plan, and a list of what the owner approves versus what you can decide alone. Then set up the guest message log and a weekly planner so nothing falls between the cracks.

Step 7: Report monthly and track your own numbers

Owners stay when they can see results. Send a simple monthly report with bookings, occupancy, income, expenses, reviews and anything you fixed. Keep your own record of income, costs and quality so you can see which clients are worth keeping.
